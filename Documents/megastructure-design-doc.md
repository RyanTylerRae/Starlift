# [Untitled] — Narrative & Level Design Doc

A first-person zero-g exploration game. A son travels to a megastructure to find his missing father, and is quietly colonized by the fungal network living inside it.

---

## Core Theme

> **You cannot find a person. You can only find what they left behind, and what they left in you.**

Expanded to a second scale:

> **Everything that loves wants to become part of something larger than itself. That's how people get lost.**

Personal reading: his father left because something out here was bigger than his family. Cosmic reading: individuality is a temporary condition — the universe is constantly dissolving discrete things into networks and systems. Same event, two magnifications.

The fungus is the engine for both. Mycelium **decomposes** (grief), **connects** (a network holding everything that died here), and **fruits** (a brief visible expression of a huge invisible body — exactly what a memory is). Play it as beautiful and unnerving, not as infection horror. Dread comes from what it implies, not from it chasing you.

---

## Core Mechanics

**Magnetic attachment + oxygen bubbles.** Charge, jump between surfaces, land, breathe. No tethers anywhere in the game.

Two mechanics carry the theme without a word of dialogue:

**Magnetism.** Metal is the father's world — grip, control, a surface to stand on. Fungal growth kills the magnetic bond. The deeper you go, the less of the built structure is available to hold, until magnetism is a luxury and the colony is the only thing you can move on.

**Bubbles.** Manufactured bubbles are safe, finite, and shrinking — the structure's dying lungs. Fungal bubbles are plentiful and dose you every time. Early on that's a real cost. By the midpoint the suit reports fungal air metabolizing *better* than the canned stuff. The game gets easier the more infected you are, and the player notices they've stopped seeking out the human air.

**The full arc in one sentence:** you start holding onto metal and rationing air, and you end up letting go and breathing freely.

### No hallucinations

Cut. Replaced by **his anchors** — magnetic pitons he left behind by hand, a physical route through the structure. Evidence instead of dream logic, and it doubles as level guidance.

Critically, the trail can *stop*. Somewhere in the back half his anchors end mid-chamber, and the only way forward is fungal. That's the moment he stopped needing the built world, delivered as a gap in a trail rather than a revelation.

## Showing the Infection

No percentage, no meter, no health bar — a number implies a fail state and does the player's noticing for them.

**1. The helmet glass (primary).** Growth creeping in from the corners of the frame, a few pixels per area. Never mentioned, never highlighted, no audio sting. Unmistakable at the edges of vision by the Gas Sea; veining across the whole visor by Fabrication. The player discovers it by realising the frame has been changing for hours.

**2. The suit's vocabulary.** Clinical throughout; only the content drifts.

| Area | Suit report |
|---|---|
| Outer Wall | "Seal integrity nominal." |
| Water Reclamation | "Foreign biological detected. Filtration nominal." |
| The Cordon | "Filtration load elevated." |
| Fuel Lines | "Filtration load elevated. Recommend servicing." |
| Gas Sea | "Filtration bypassed." |
| Cooling | "Filtration offline. Reserve atmosphere only." |
| Fabrication | "Oxygen synthesis: internal source." |
| The Bloom | *(silence — nothing left it considers abnormal)* |

The Fabrication line is the reveal, with no drama attached.

**3. Filter life (optional).** One consumable counting *down* — hours remaining, the readout every survival player instinctively dreads. It runs out around Cooling. And nothing happens. No suffocation, no damage; the suit just stops mentioning it. The thing the player spent six hours protecting turns out not to have been load-bearing.

Lead with 1 and 2. Add 3 only if the player needs something concrete to plan around. All three at full volume over-explains it.

**Rule:** the drone never comments on the player's condition. It's the only other voice in the game, and its silence about the obvious thing is worth more than any warning.

## The Eight Areas

### 1. Outer Wall — *no new mechanic*
**Function:** Hull, docking spars, external sensor masts.
**Look:** Clean engineered plate going on forever. The structure never fully resolves — you can't back up far enough to see its shape.
**Theme:** Arrival. You are very small.
**Mechanic:** Teach the loop — charge, jump, land, breathe. Bare hull, clean grip, manufactured bubbles at regular intervals.
**Father:** *The call.* His first anchor is bolted beside the airlock — a hand-placed piton where none should be, dated years before anything went wrong. He arrived as contracted maintenance, on rotation, entirely ordinary. His docking clamp is already open.

### 2. Water Reclamation — *water spheres*
**Function:** Purification, ice storage, reservoir spheres.
**Look:** House-sized spheres of water drifting free. Sound goes muffled and warm for three seconds every time you pass through one.
**Theme:** Baptism. The first pleasant thing in the game is the thing that infects you.
**Mechanic:** Entering a sphere kills momentum and breaks magnetism. Line up a jump *through* one to slow down before a hard landing. The center of a large sphere is breathable. The first fungal bubble sits beside a manufactured one — entirely optional, taken once, and it starts everything.
**Father:** *The ordinary world.* Crew, routines, a mug, a shift roster with his name on it. Mentions of home get shorter with each log until they stop.

### 3. The Cordon — *mines*
**Function:** Sectional bulkheads, blast doors, automated defense.
**Look:** A minefield in zero-g — proximity charges drifting slowly, all facing inward. Welded doors. Someone sealed this from the outside.
**Theme:** Containment, and its failure.
**Mechanic:** Mines trigger on magnetic signature, so landing on metal is what kills you. Cross on fungal growth instead — the first time the infection is the solution. The drone can pull individual mines off-line, one at a time, slowly.
**Father:** *The refusal.* He sealed this. His signature is on the containment order. He begins the story as the fungus's enemy — which is what gives him somewhere to travel from. And he ended up on the wrong side of his own door.

### 4. Fuel Lines — *breakable surfaces*
**Function:** Propellant transfer, pump housings, feed tubing.
**Look:** Kilometres of black pipe. Unburned fuel pooled into wobbling spheres. Flame burns as slow blue balls instead of flickering upward.
**Theme:** The vein. You're inside something's circulatory system.
**Mechanic:** Corroded pipework gives way on hard landings. A full-power jump punches through and vents you into the next chamber; a soft landing holds. Reading a surface before committing is the whole skill. No manufactured bubbles at all — fuel line, not air line.
**Father:** *The trials.* He went back in, alone, unsanctioned, over and over. His anchors are densest here; he ran this route for months.

### 5. The Gas Sea — *drag and open crossings*
**Function:** Ventilation trunk / atmospheric processing, gone feral.
**Look:** The set piece. A chamber the size of a bay, filled with gas thick enough to swim in, lit from somewhere far below. Fungal medusae drift through it in schools — translucent spore-sacs with fins, indifferent to you.
**Theme:** A world that grew inside the machine and doesn't know the machine is there.
**Mechanic:** Real drag; you swim. Nothing to grip in the open — aim at the far wall and commit, and a bad angle leaves you drifting short. The gas is breathable and saturated: the biggest jump in infection happens during the most beautiful thing in the game.
**Father:** *The revelation.* Whatever he saw among the medusae, the log he leaves here is the last one that sounds like a man addressing other people.

### 6. Cooling — *shrinking bubbles*
**Function:** Radiator fins, heat rejection, thermal loop.
**Look:** Black fins the length of a city, edge-on to deep space. Nothing grows here. The colour drops out of the game.
**Theme:** Clarity, and what it costs.
**Mechanic:** Perfect magnetic grip, nothing growing, manufactured air only — and reserves are nearly gone, so each bubble contracts while you're in it. Route planning under a clock. It plays like Area 1 and it's punishing now, because you've spent hours forgetting how to ration.
**Father:** *The abyss.* He came out to the fins because nothing grows in the cold and he wanted to think clearly. His suit is still there — intact, undamaged, opened from the inside. He thought clearly and chose.

### 7. Fabrication — *grown bubbles*
**Function:** Assembly bays, printers, parts stock.
**Look:** Manufacturing arms grown over with mycelium, still running, still making things. Racks of half-finished shapes that are almost bodies and almost drones.
**Theme:** What it wants. It doesn't consume — it produces.
**Mechanic:** Touch a growth node and a bubble blooms where you need it. You build your own route out of the colony now.
**Father:** *The elixir.* He designed the drone, set the signal, and sent it home in his place. It was made from his pattern on this line.

### 8. The Bloom — *no mechanic*
**Function:** Core. Originally the reactor gallery; now the fruiting chamber.
**Look:** The cathedral. Every dead thing the structure ever held, distributed and lit.
**Theme:** The choice.
**Mechanic:** No magnetism, no bubble management, no jumping puzzle. Continuous air for the first time. Everything the game taught you is switched off, and you simply move.
**Father:** *The return that never happened.* The final log.

## The Father

The monomyth with one stage missing: he gets the call, crosses the threshold, endures the trials, and comes back with something — and then doesn't come back. The player is walking a hero's journey that stalled one beat from the end. Which reframes the son: he isn't the hero of a rescue, he's the missing final stage of somebody else's story.

### Told through his work, not his writing

The strongest evidence in a game about a maintenance engineer is the maintenance. Track his conversion in the repairs:

- **Early:** neat, to regulation, sealing the structure against intrusion.
- **Middle:** improvised, hurried, still defensive.
- **Late:** he's no longer patching holes, he's cutting them. Ports opened between sections. Grating removed. Conduits rerouted to carry spores where they couldn't reach.
- **Fabrication:** not repairing the structure at all. Assisting it.

A player who never reads a single log will still understand the man from the welds.

**His kit** is the second tell. He leaves gear behind at every stage — tools, canisters, cutting rig, finally the suit. An inventory that shrinks to nothing. He gave up the built world one object at a time.

### What keeps him human

Give him a reason that isn't enlightenment. **The Cordon killed people, on his order, and he lived with it.** Then he found evidence that they hadn't ended so much as continued — and a man in that position will believe almost anything rather than the alternative.

Never resolve whether he found the truth or built the only story he could survive. That ambiguity is what makes the three endings equal weight.

### He never speaks

No voice in the Bloom. A **final log**, found where he'd have set it deliberately — an anchor at the edge of the chamber, the last thing he bolted down.

It should not explain the colony, apologise, or address the son. Best version: it isn't addressed to anyone. A man describing what he's about to do in flat practical language, the way you'd write a maintenance note. The love is in the fact that he left it where only someone following his anchors would find it.

### Optional layer

A second set of anchors in the deepest sections — older, a different pattern, not his. He was following someone too.

## The Twist

The megastructure is not a station and not a ruin. It's the **fruiting body of a civilization that already made his choice** — a species that decided individuality was a phase and dissolved into the network. The colony isn't native to the structure. The colony *is* the structure's builders, in their final form.

So the father's great transcendent decision isn't special. It's **typical** — what happens everywhere, given enough time. The gut-punch isn't that the universe is cruel; it's that a specific, personal, unbearable loss is a common event at cosmic frequency. Grief doesn't get to be unique either.

The counterweight, and where the hope lives: across everything that ever lived here, **the son is the only thing that came back for someone.** Attachment to one particular person is statistically absurd, thermodynamically doomed, and appears to be rare. That's what makes it worth anything.

The son was also seeded before he ever docked — the drone was built from the father's pattern and the distress signal came off the Fabrication line. Not malice. He wanted to give his son something, and this was the only form he had left.

---

## Endings

Three, none marked correct, nothing prompting the choice.

- **Burn it.** The particular over the universal. One man mattered more than a civilization's afterlife. Monstrous and completely understandable.
- **Leave.** Accept the scale, keep your shape, carry the loss home. The lonely, adult ending.
- **Open the helmet.** Agree with him. Join. The camera keeps going after you stop.

Because the father's motive is unresolvable, all three stay live: burn it and he was a deluded murderer, leave and it stays unknowable, join and he was right all along.

Hold the last shot long enough that the player notices the structure doesn't react. It was never about them.

---

## Craft Notes

- **Environmental storytelling only.** No exposition dumps, no explaining the colony's biology. Underexplain deliberately.
- **Area 6 (Cooling) is the argument's weak point exposed on purpose** — a taste of what going home would actually feel like — so the final choice isn't rigged.
- **The drone** is the only other voice. Never let it comment on the player's condition. Consider making the last traversal one it can't follow.
- **Never signal significance.** Show the impossible with the same flat confidence as everything else.
